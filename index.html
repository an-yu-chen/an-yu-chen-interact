01 · THE PROBLEM

A technology is evaluated differently depending on who is looking.

A scientific result can be a breakthrough, a patent, a business opportunity, a strategic capability or a societal solution — without the underlying technology changing. Research on technology transfer and academic entrepreneurship likewise shows that university knowledge moves through multiple organisational, institutional and commercial settings rather than through a single linear pathway (Wright et al., 2004; Hayter et al., 2018; Abreu & Grinevich, 2024).

The missing piece is the trajectory of meaning: how an opportunity is framed, questioned, translated and revised as it crosses institutional boundaries. This emphasis connects the project to research on framing, boundary work and the interpretive work performed by technology-transfer professionals (Pan et al., 2020; Sapir, 2021). Research on innovation intermediation also shows that intermediaries do more than pass knowledge between parties: they search, broker, translate, validate and help connect actors whose knowledge and goals differ (Howells, 2006; Howells & Thomas, 2022). The project therefore treats these boundary encounters as part of the transfer process itself, not as administrative steps around it. This also matters for policy: technology-transfer performance depends partly on organisational practices and the indicators used to assess offices, so outcomes should be interpreted in relation to institutional settings rather than as context-free measures of performance (Siegel et al., 2003).

CORE QUESTION

When does translation create legitimacy and resources — and when can it hide technical uncertainty? This question builds on evidence that linguistic framing can shape resource acquisition while evaluation remains sensitive to audience and context (Pan et al., 2020; van Werven et al., 2019).

03 · RESEARCH DESIGN

Follow the opportunity — not just the technology.

The unit of analysis is a technology-opportunity trajectory: one identifiable high-tech opportunity followed from research origin through successive evaluation, transfer and commercialisation encounters. This longitudinal orientation responds to literature showing that university technology transfer is embedded in evolving ecosystems, organisational roles and entrepreneurial processes (Wright et al., 2004; Hayter et al., 2018; Abreu & Grinevich, 2024; Abid et al., 2025). It also avoids treating transfer as a single patenting or licensing event: commercialisation can involve iterative proof-of-concept work, stakeholder integration and the gradual development of absorptive capacity on the receiving side (McAdam et al., 2009).

How the evidence is built: first identify and reconstruct trajectories; then locate evaluation events; then analyse the language and interaction around those events; finally connect changes in opportunity accounts with observable transfer outcomes and experimentally test selected framing effects.

Data sources

EPO Deep Tech Finder / PATSTAT, OpenAlex, CORDIS, patents, publications, grants, TTO materials, company records and investment announcements. PATSTAT provides bibliographic, legal-event and procedural patent data; CORDIS provides EU-funded project, participant, deliverable and publication records; and OpenAlex links scholarly works to authors, institutions, funders and topics (European Patent Office, 2026; European Commission, 2026; OpenAlex, 2026).

What happens

Identify the technology, related organisations and dated events, then connect research, intellectual property, funding, company, investment, collaboration, licensing and pilot records.

Output

A dated trajectory for each case showing the technology, actors, evidence sources and major transfer/evaluation milestones.

Sampling target: approximately 30–40 trajectories selected through a stratified technology portfolio: AI, software & intelligent systems; biotechnology, health & biomedical technologies; photonics, advanced materials & manufacturing; and energy, climate & resource-efficient technologies. The families are comparative anchors rather than rigid sectors, allowing cross-domain convergence and application movement to remain visible (Haessler et al., 2023; OECD, 2025). The proposal defines one technology opportunity followed from research origin through subsequent evaluation, transfer and commercialisation events as the unit of analysis.

Case identification and selection: The initial opportunity universe will be constructed using explicit inclusion criteria and a documented entity-resolution protocol linking publications, patent families, projects, organisations and commercialisation events. Ambiguous matches will be flagged rather than automatically merged, with matching decisions, confidence levels and source provenance retained for audit. From this universe, trajectories will be selected to support comparison across technology families, application contexts and transfer stages, including contrasting cases in which opportunities progress, stall, are redirected or are abandoned. This combines systematic case construction with the contextual depth needed to explain heterogeneous technology-transfer processes (Cunningham et al., 2017; Beach, 2020).

What is collected

Time-stamped text from patents, publications, grants, TTO materials, venture/company pages, investment announcements and other accessible communications.

Evaluation events

TTO assessments, grant decisions, investor pitches, industry negotiations, pilots, licensing decisions and other moments where the opportunity is evaluated. TTOs can act as brokers across academic and industrial boundaries while also managing institutional and ownership constraints (Sapir, 2021; Chen et al., 2025).

What is coded

For each event: problem, value, evidence, uncertainty and the requested response. This keeps evaluation tied to observable interaction rather than treating framing as a purely rhetorical property (van Werven et al., 2019; Clarke & Llewellyn, 2026).

Key principle: the project uses contemporaneous, time-stamped material wherever available so that changes in an opportunity account can be observed rather than reconstructed only from hindsight.

Three dimensions

Meaning: what the technology is understood to be. Value: why it is worth supporting or adopting. Uncertainty: what remains unknown or unvalidated.

Computational analysis

Transformer-based text representations and comparison of language patterns, complemented by transparent dictionary-based measures. The design is informed by research demonstrating how linguistic and narrative features can be analysed systematically in entrepreneurial settings (Pan et al., 2020; Oo et al., 2023).

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative coding and process tracing are used to interpret why language changes and to examine the context behind computational patterns.

Methodological logic: computational analysis identifies recurring or changing patterns; transparent coding and qualitative process tracing make those patterns interpretable and tied to specific evaluation episodes.

Interview sample

Approximately 45–60 interviews with researchers, entrepreneurs, TTO professionals, investors and industry partners. This multi-actor design reflects evidence that technology-transfer work involves brokerage, legitimacy management and competing organisational expectations across boundaries (O’Kane et al., 2015; Sapir, 2021).

Episode reconstruction

Link interview accounts and accessible interaction records to specific evaluation events identified in the trajectory.

Analytical question

Who challenged, accepted, questioned or revised an opportunity account — and what evidence or response followed? Recent research on investment pitches similarly treats evaluation as an interaction in which investors challenge and redirect entrepreneurial narratives rather than simply receiving a fixed story (Clarke & Llewellyn, 2026).

Contribution: this step moves beyond observing that language changed. It reconstructs the interactional process through which an opportunity was interpreted and potentially reconfigured.

Design

A controlled vignette experiment presents the same underlying technical opportunity with systematically varied representations. This follows evidence that alternative linguistic and narrative frames can influence how entrepreneurial opportunities are interpreted and resourced (Pan et al., 2020; Purtell et al., 2024).

Stakeholder groups

The pilot design targets approximately 180–240 participants across 3–4 stakeholder groups.

Measures

Perceived value, legitimacy, risk, uncertainty and willingness to engage.

Purpose: the experiment complements the longitudinal cases by testing whether selected differences in representation are associated with different evaluations while the underlying technical opportunity is held constant.

Observable outcomes

Funding, collaboration, licensing, pilots, spin-off development and abandonment, alongside patents, company and investment events where relevant.

Temporal linkage

Align changes in opportunity accounts and evaluation episodes with dated subsequent resource, relationship and transfer events.

Interpretation

Assess how patterns of reconfiguration are associated with subsequent trajectories without assuming that framing alone causes those outcomes.

Core question: under what conditions does adaptation improve audience relevance while preserving decision-relevant technological uncertainty?

04 · WORK PACKAGES

One evidence architecture, six connected work packages.

The work packages operationalise the existing research design without replacing it: WP1 builds the opportunity universe and common data spine; WP2 reconstructs trajectories; WP3 explains evaluation interactions; WP4 measures patterns across the corpus; WP5 tests selected framing effects experimentally; and WP6 integrates the evidence into a longitudinal diagnostic.

WP1 · DATA FOUNDATION

Build the opportunity universe and data spine

Core question: What constitutes a traceable high-tech opportunity, and which observable records allow it to be followed across institutional boundaries?

Create the reproducible population from which trajectories are sampled and establish a common identifier system linking research, intellectual property, organisations, funding, firms and investment events.

Data integration and quality assurance. Record linkage will combine stable identifiers where available with documented matching rules for organisation names, inventor and author affiliations, patent families, project identifiers and company records. Each link will retain its source, matching method, confidence level and any manual review decision. The project will distinguish missing information from confirmed absence of an event and will maintain versioned records of extraction, cleaning, classification and coding. These procedures will make the opportunity register reproducible and allow uncertain links to be revisited as additional evidence becomes available.

  • Use EPO PATSTAT for patent families, applicants, inventors, classifications and legal/procedural events.
  • Use the EPO Deep Tech Finder as a discovery and cross-check layer, while retaining project-level entity resolution and provenance.
  • Use CORDIS for EU-funded projects, participating organisations, collaborations and project results.
  • Use OpenAlex and institutional repositories for publication metadata, authorship, affiliations, citations and topic representations.
  • Classify each candidate by a primary technology family and retain secondary technology tags to capture convergence.
  • Separately classify application/industry context so that technology and market setting are not conflated.
OutputMaster opportunity register with persistent IDs, provenance fields, confidence scores, technology-family tags, industry/application tags and dated event spine.
SamplingBroad VUB/Belgian-linked candidate universe → approximately 30–40 trajectories, stratified across four technology families and three priority industry/application contexts.
Priority industry/application focus: healthcare & medtech; digital/enterprise technologies; and advanced industrial/manufacturing systems, including energy/resource-efficient applications where they enter these industrial settings. The four technology families remain AI/software/intelligent systems; biotechnology/health/biomedical technologies; photonics/advanced materials/manufacturing; and energy/climate/resource-efficient technologies. The design follows the technology-focused commercialisation literature while treating industry context as a boundary condition rather than equating technology with industry (Haessler et al., 2023; Hoppmann et al., 2020; Bojovic, 2022).
WP2 · LONGITUDINAL RECONSTRUCTION

Reconstruct technology-opportunity trajectories

Core question: How does the same opportunity acquire different meanings, value claims and uncertainty profiles as it travels through research, TTO, investor and industry settings, and does this reconfiguration vary by technology family or application context?

Turn heterogeneous records into event-level histories showing when an opportunity moved between communities of evaluation, what each community asked for, and what changed afterwards.

  • Collect publications, patents, grant applications/results, TTO materials, company descriptions, accessible pitch/investor materials, licensing or partnership announcements and pilot descriptions.
  • Record claim, audience, evidence, uncertainty, requested action and subsequent response for each item, with separate fields for technology family and application/industry context.
  • Keep publication date and event date separate to avoid retrospective accounts being treated as contemporaneous evidence.
  • Assign source reliability and access status.
Output30–40 validated trajectories and an event-level corpus.
FeedsWP3 mechanism analysis and WP4 corpus-wide measurement.
WP3 · MECHANISMS

Explain meaning-making at evaluation boundaries

Core question: Who changes an opportunity account, why does the change occur, and which questions or evidence requirements trigger the revision?

Use interviews and close qualitative reconstruction to explain the interactional mechanisms behind observed changes rather than treating computational language differences as self-explanatory. The analysis will also examine the documents, prototypes, evidence summaries and other boundary objects through which actors make an opportunity sufficiently understandable to evaluate, while recognising that shared artefacts do not automatically create shared interpretations (Caccamo et al., 2023).

Mechanism-focused analysis. Each selected episode will be reconstructed by comparing contemporaneous documents, interview accounts and observable subsequent responses. The analysis will trace the sequence from an initial opportunity claim, through a stakeholder question or objection, to any evidence request, revision of the opportunity account and subsequent evaluation response. Rival explanations—including changes in technical maturity, available resources, organisational priorities or external market conditions—will be considered alongside framing-related explanations. This process-tracing approach will help distinguish plausible mechanisms from simple temporal association (Beach, 2020).

  • Sample approximately 45–60 researchers, entrepreneurs, TTO professionals, investors and industry partners from WP2 trajectories.
  • Use event-specific prompts anchored to identifiable evaluation episodes.
  • Connect participant-provided documents to trajectory records through study-specific event IDs.
  • Compare successful, delayed, redirected and abandoned trajectories across technology families and the priority industry/application contexts.
OutputMechanism-focused case reconstructions.
Analytical dimensionsMeaning · value · uncertainty · actor · audience · evidence request · response · outcome.
WP4 · COMPUTATIONAL COMPARISON

Measure patterns of framing, value and uncertainty at scale

Core question: Which linguistic and evidential patterns distinguish representations of the same opportunity across evaluation communities, technology families and application/industry contexts?

Move from close case reconstruction to systematic comparison across the corpus, testing whether opportunity accounts change predictably across audiences and stages of transfer.

Measurement validation. Computational measures will be developed and validated against a manually coded subset of documents sampled across technology families, document types, stakeholder audiences and trajectory stages. The coding framework will define observable indicators for problem framing, value claims, evidence, uncertainty and requested action. Where coding is conducted by multiple researchers, agreement will be assessed on an overlapping subset and disagreements used to refine the codebook. Model outputs will be evaluated against human-coded examples and close readings, with sensitivity checks across alternative measures and document subsets. Automated text analysis will extend systematic comparison across the corpus, not substitute for contextual interpretation (Grimmer & Stewart, 2013; O’Connor & Joffe, 2020). If structural topic modelling is included as an exploratory method, it can provide an additional way to examine how topic prevalence varies with document-level metadata (Roberts et al., 2019).

  • Combine transformer-based text embeddings with transparent dictionary and supervised coding measures.
  • Compare documents within trajectories over time and across the three priority industry/application contexts, rather than only across unrelated firms.
  • Link language shifts to boundary crossings, evidence requests, investment events, partnerships, pilots and licensing.
  • Run sensitivity checks across document type, technology field, trajectory length, missingness and alternative definitions of frame adaptation.
OutputTransparent computational measurement framework with reproducible preprocessing and robustness checks.
GuardrailComputational similarity identifies patterns; it does not by itself establish mechanism or causality.
WP5 · EXPERIMENT

Test framing effects with controlled stakeholder experiments

Core question: When the underlying technical opportunity is held constant, does opportunity framing alter stakeholder evaluation, and does the effect depend on the technology family or application/industry context?

The principal independent variable is opportunity framing: how the same technical opportunity is represented in terms of problem, value, evidence, uncertainty, novelty/familiarity and application. The primary dependent variables are stakeholder evaluation: perceived value, legitimacy, technical risk, uncertainty, evidence sufficiency and willingness to engage. Investment, collaboration, piloting and licensing intentions are secondary behavioural outcomes. This follows framing research showing that linguistic and cognitive frames can shape innovation adoption, investor response and the interpretation of emerging technologies (Pan et al., 2020; Raffaelli et al., 2019; Spieth et al., 2021; Snihur et al., 2022; Taeuscher & Rothe, 2024).

Experimental implementation and analysis. Framing conditions will be derived from patterns observed in WP3–WP4 and will vary theoretically specified features of the opportunity account while preserving the underlying technical facts and evidence base. The study will use random assignment, pre-specified primary outcomes and manipulation checks to assess whether participants perceive the intended differences between conditions. The analysis will account for stakeholder-group differences and, where participants evaluate multiple vignettes, the dependence of observations within participants and scenarios. The pilot will assess recruitment feasibility, comprehension, measure performance and treatment differentiation before the confirmatory design is finalised (Aguinis & Bradley, 2014; Baguley et al., 2022).

  • Build treatments directly from empirically observed WP3–WP4 framing patterns.
  • Randomly assign alternative representations of the same technical opportunity while holding technical facts, maturity, evidence base and core functionality constant.
  • Measure value, legitimacy, technical risk, uncertainty, evidence sufficiency and willingness to collaborate, invest or pilot as appropriate; treat application/industry context as a theoretically motivated moderator rather than a second treatment unless the design explicitly tests an interaction.
  • Pre-register hypotheses, exclusions and primary outcomes before confirmatory analysis.
Planned sampleApproximately 180–240 participants across 3–4 stakeholder groups, with technology/application context balanced where feasible.
IdentificationIsolates representation effects under controlled conditions; does not claim framing is the sole determinant of real-world transfer.
WP6 · INTEGRATION & DIAGNOSTIC

Link reconfiguration to transfer trajectories and build the diagnostic

Core question: Under what conditions is adaptation associated with subsequent transfer outcomes—funding, collaboration, licensing, piloting or spin-off development—without erasing decision-relevant uncertainty?

Integrate trajectory events, qualitative mechanisms, computational measures and experimental estimates into a common evidence matrix. Interpretation will distinguish multiple routes to knowledge transfer and commercialisation, rather than assuming that patenting, licensing or spin-off creation alone captures the full value of university–industry engagement (Grimaldi et al., 2011; Howells, 2006).

Temporal and outcome interpretation. The analysis will distinguish the timing of framing changes from the timing of subsequent funding, collaboration, licensing, piloting and other transfer events. Where the number and quality of observations permit, trajectories will be compared by outcome type and stage, while accounting for differences in technology maturity, application context and evidence availability. Because successful transfer can take multiple forms and outcomes may be incompletely observed, the diagnostic will record both realised outcomes and unresolved or censored trajectories. Associations between framing adaptation and subsequent transfer will be interpreted in light of alternative explanations rather than as evidence of a standalone causal effect.

  • Align frame-change dates with subsequent funding, investment, collaboration, licensing, pilots, spin-off development and abandonment where observable, treating these as longitudinal transfer outcomes rather than direct experimental DVs.
  • Use temporal ordering to distinguish prior representations from later outcomes.
  • Compare trajectories with similar technologies or stages where feasible, including cases where translation occurred without a corresponding transfer outcome.
  • Produce a diagnostic recording audience, opportunity claim, evidence supplied, unresolved uncertainty, frame change, evaluation response and subsequent outcome.
OutputTechnology Opportunity Translation Diagnostic, papers, anonymised coding framework, stakeholder/policy brief and reusable dataset structure.
Final yearReplication checks, stakeholder validation, dissemination and data/code documentation.
Work-package logic

WP1 establishes the identifiers, technology-family classification and three-industry/application sampling frame. WP2 turns those identifiers into longitudinal event histories. WP3 explains how evaluation interactions produce revisions. WP4 tests whether framing, value and uncertainty patterns vary systematically within trajectories, across technology families and across application contexts. WP5 tests the causal effect of opportunity framing on stakeholder evaluation under controlled conditions. WP6 reconnects those experimental effects to longitudinal transfer outcomes without claiming that framing alone causes commercialisation (Haessler et al., 2023; Reynolds et al., 2025; Bunduchi & Candi, 2026).

05 · DATA & EVIDENCE ARCHITECTURE

A common spine keeps every layer traceable.

The entity, technology, interaction, text, outcome and provenance layers are not separate datasets. They are linked views of the same opportunity trajectory, joined through persistent identifiers and dated events. This longitudinal, multi-source design reflects the need to study technology transfer as an evolving process involving multiple actors and organisational boundaries rather than as a single transfer event (Dolmans et al., 2022; Haessler et al., 2023).

RESEARCH MODEL · IV / DV / CONTEXT

Framing is the intervention; stakeholder evaluation is the immediate outcome; transfer is the longitudinal outcome.

The project distinguishes the representation of an opportunity from what stakeholders subsequently evaluate and from what happens to the opportunity over time. This prevents the empirical design from treating “commercialisation” as a single short-term dependent variable.

Independent variableOpportunity framing
Problem, value, evidence, uncertainty, novelty/familiarity and application framing.
MechanismStakeholder sensemaking / evaluation
How actors interpret value, legitimacy, evidence sufficiency, risk and uncertainty.
Primary DVStakeholder evaluation and response
Perceived value, legitimacy, risk, uncertainty, evidence sufficiency and willingness to invest, collaborate or pilot.
Longitudinal DVTransfer trajectory
Funding, investment, collaboration, licensing, pilots, spin-off development, redirection or abandonment.
Industry/application context as moderator: the study focuses on three priority contexts—healthcare & medtech, digital/enterprise technologies, and advanced industrial/manufacturing systems. Technology family and technology maturity are additional contextual dimensions. The expectation is not that framing has one universal effect, but that its consequences depend on the evidentiary, institutional and market conditions in which an opportunity is evaluated (Pan et al., 2020; Bojovic, 2022; Raffaelli et al., 2019; Taeuscher & Rothe, 2024).
CORE TESTABLE RELATION

Holding technical content, maturity and evidence constant, different opportunity frames should change stakeholder evaluation. The magnitude and direction of this effect may vary by application context and technology characteristics; longitudinally, frame adaptation is expected to be associated with subsequent transfer trajectories when it responds to legitimate evidence and stakeholder requirements rather than merely suppressing uncertainty.

07 · CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

Technology-opportunity trajectories: meaning, value and uncertainty in motion.

The project treats opportunity value as something that can be reinterpreted through successive evaluation encounters rather than as a fixed property of a technology. This is consistent with research showing that entrepreneurial and commercialisation work involves framing, narrative negotiation, boundary spanning, changing regimes of value and intermediary roles across academic and industrial settings (Pan et al., 2020; van Werven et al., 2019; Mäkinen & Sapir, 2023; Dolmans et al., 2022; O’Kane et al., 2015).

09 · WHY INTERACT / WHY VUB

The project is designed around InterACT’s interdisciplinary and intersectoral architecture.

Excellence

Connects framing, phenomenology, entrepreneurship, technology transfer, computational text analysis and longitudinal case research.

Impact

Moves from abstract framing effects to measurable transfer outcomes and a stakeholder-facing diagnostic.

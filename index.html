01 · THE PROBLEM

A technology is evaluated differently depending on who is looking.

A scientific result can be a breakthrough, a patent, a business opportunity, a strategic capability or a societal solution — without the underlying technology changing.

The missing piece is the trajectory of meaning: how an opportunity is framed, questioned, translated and revised as it crosses institutional boundaries.

CORE QUESTION

When does translation create legitimacy and resources — and when can it hide technical uncertainty?

03 · RESEARCH DESIGN

From “framing” to a measurable trajectory.

01

Map

Identify technology trajectories using EPO Deep Tech Finder, PATSTAT, OpenAlex and CORDIS, complemented by company and investment records.

02

Extract

Collect time-stamped language from patents, papers, grants, venture pages, press releases and investor communications.

03

Compare

Measure novelty, familiarity, market, scientific, societal and risk frames using transformer-based embeddings plus transparent dictionary and qualitative coding.

04

Trace

Pair text changes with interviews and interaction records to reconstruct who challenged, accepted or revised an opportunity account.

05

Test

Run a controlled vignette experiment presenting the same technology with systematically varied frames to stakeholder groups.

06

Link

Relate framing trajectories to observable outcomes: funding, partnerships, licensing, patents, venture formation, pilots and abandonment.

TESTABLE PROPOSITION

Frame adaptation should matter most at boundary-crossing moments. The key empirical question is whether adaptation improves access to resources without reducing the visibility of technical uncertainty.

08 · LITERATURE ANCHORS

The proposal updates the literature to 2026.

  1. Crispeels, De Moortel, Gruber & Guerrero (2026). University technology-transfer ecosystems should be studied through the micro-foundations of who is involved, how processes are navigated and where this takes place.
  2. Clarke & Llewellyn (2026). Investment pitching is an interactional process in which stories are scrutinised, challenged and negotiated — directly motivating RQ2.
  3. Siegel (2026). Recent technology-transfer scholarship calls for more micro-level research and greater attention to pro-social technology transfer.
  4. Johnson, Hughes & Huang (2026). A systematic review of 122 papers highlights the need to connect university–industry collaboration, capital resources and firm-level processes.
  5. Pan et al. (2020). Evidence from 2,883 US IT ventures and 5,849 investment events shows that novelty and familiarity frames are consequential in venture funding.

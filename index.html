Responsible Innovation: How Technology Opportunities Gain, Lose and Reconfigure Value Across Communities of Evaluation

Responsible innovation is approached as a process in which technology opportunities are interpreted, evaluated and revalued across different communities, as expectations encounter evidence and uncertainty. The project follows researchers, technology-transfer professionals, entrepreneurs, investors, industry partners and policy actors to understand how changes in meaning and perceived value relate to demonstrated capability and subsequent technology-transfer outcomes (Borup et al., 2006; Stilgoe et al., 2013; Spieth et al., 2021).

REAL-WORLD MOTIVATION · EXPECTATIONS MEET EVIDENCE

When investment expectations move faster than technology adoption.

Recent clean-energy and battery-industry events show why responsible evaluation matters in practice.

On 27 June 2025, Reuters Breakingviews columnist Edward Chancellor argued that “Both frenzies were driven by investors’ unrealistic expectations about how quickly new energy technologies would be taken up” and that “The common error investors made during both booms was to become entranced by extravagant growth forecasts” (Chancellor, 2025). His account of successive clean-technology investment booms illustrates how expectations about adoption can outpace the evidence available about the pace and conditions of technological change.

Earlier, on 22 November 2024, Reuters reported that Northvolt had attracted substantial investment and had become central to European ambitions for a home-grown electric-vehicle battery industry. Yet production difficulties, financial pressure and slower-than-expected EV demand challenged expectations about its commercial potential (Mannes et al., 2024). This case should not be read as proof that framing alone caused Northvolt’s difficulties; it illustrates why technological capability, scale-up, market demand and financing assumptions need to be evaluated together.

THE RESEARCH PROBLEM

While technology developers frame potential, prospects, performance and progress to influence stakeholder expectations (Hoppmann et al., 2020), opportunities are also evaluated by investors, universities, industry partners, public funders and policy actors. How do these communities interpret and revalue the same opportunity as expectations encounter evidence and uncertainty?

Responsible innovation means distinguishing what has been demonstrated from what remains a plausible future possibility, while examining how those distinctions shape decisions about further research, pilots, investment, licensing and knowledge transfer (Stilgoe et al., 2013; Bozeman et al., 2015).

Sources: Reuters Breakingviews, 26/27 June 2025; Reuters report republished by Investing.com, 22 November 2024.

01 · THE PROBLEM

Technology opportunities gain, lose and reconfigure value across communities of evaluation.

A scientific result can be a breakthrough, a patent, a business opportunity, a strategic capability or a societal solution — without the underlying technology changing. Research on technology transfer and academic entrepreneurship likewise shows that university knowledge moves through multiple organisational, institutional and commercial settings, not through a single linear pathway (Wright et al., 2004; Hayter et al., 2018; Abreu & Grinevich, 2024).

The missing piece is a longitudinal account of how meaning, perceived value and available evidence change—and how these dimensions relate without being treated as interchangeable. The project examines the technology-opportunity trajectory: how an opportunity is framed, questioned, translated and revised as it crosses institutional boundaries. This emphasis connects the project to research on framing as a process of selecting and emphasising aspects of a situation (Benford & Snow, 2000), entrepreneurial framing in investor evaluation (Pan et al., 2020), and the interpretive work performed by technology-transfer professionals (Sapir, 2021). Research on innovation intermediation shows that intermediaries search, broker, translate, validate and connect actors whose knowledge and goals differ (Howells, 2006; Howells & Thomas, 2022). The project treats these encounters as part of knowledge valorisation itself, consistent with European guidance that valorisation extends beyond formal intellectual-property management to the use of research results and other intellectual assets (European Commission, 2023). It asks how universities, industry partners and public innovation actors can assess opportunities when evidence is incomplete, and how they can distinguish evidence-responsive adaptation from changes in presentation that obscure unresolved uncertainty. This connects the empirical research to institutional practice and evidence-informed policy without assuming that commercialisation is the only valuable outcome (Siegel et al., 2003).

Responsible Innovation provides a framework for examining how anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion and responsiveness can be incorporated into the evaluation and governance of emerging technologies (Owen et al., 2012; Stilgoe et al., 2013).

However, responsible innovation scholarship also raises questions about how these principles are enacted in practice, particularly when innovation involves competing interpretations, uncertain futures and institutional constraints (Genus & Iskandarova, 2018; Smolka & Böschen, 2023).

Responsible innovation and innovation-policy research highlight the importance of considering societal purposes, uncertainties and transformative outcomes alongside conventional innovation objectives (Owen et al., 2012; Weber & Rohracher, 2012; Schot & Steinmueller, 2018).

OVERARCHING RESEARCH QUESTION

How do technology opportunities gain, lose and reconfigure value across communities of evaluation, and what are the implications for responsible innovation and technology transfer under uncertainty?

RQ1 · Evaluation: How do different communities interpret the same high-tech opportunity, and which technological, commercial or societal frames shape their evaluations?

RQ2 · Interaction: How do researchers and entrepreneurs adapt their accounts of an opportunity when communicating across investor, university, industry and policy settings?

RQ3 · Consequences: When does this adaptation help an opportunity gain legitimacy, resources and partners, and when might it obscure important technological uncertainty? These questions build on research showing that framing can shape resource acquisition while evaluation remains sensitive to audience and context (Pan et al., 2020; van Werven et al., 2019; Clarke & Llewellyn, 2026).

03 · RESEARCH DESIGN

Follow the opportunity — not just the technology.

The unit of analysis is a technology-opportunity trajectory: one identifiable high-tech opportunity followed from research origin through successive evaluation, transfer and commercialisation encounters. This longitudinal orientation responds to literature showing that university technology transfer is embedded in evolving ecosystems, organisational roles and entrepreneurial processes (Wright et al., 2004; Hayter et al., 2018; Abreu & Grinevich, 2024; Abid et al., 2025). Research also suggests that technology attributes, such as prototype maturity, clarity of market segments and regulatory hurdles, inform entrepreneurs’ licensing decisions (Brown et al., 2022). It also avoids treating transfer as a single patenting or licensing event: commercialisation can involve iterative proof-of-concept work, stakeholder integration and the gradual development of absorptive capacity on the receiving side (McAdam et al., 2009).

How the data and evidence will be built: first identify and reconstruct trajectories; then locate evaluation events; then analyse the language and interaction around those events; finally connect changes in opportunity accounts with observable transfer outcomes, using qualitative process tracing to explain the context behind computational patterns.

Data sources

EPO Deep Tech Finder / PATSTAT, OpenAlex, CORDIS, patents, publications, grants, TTO materials, company records and investment announcements. PATSTAT provides bibliographic, legal-event and procedural patent data; CORDIS provides EU-funded project, participant, deliverable and publication records; and OpenAlex links scholarly works to authors, institutions, funders and topics (European Patent Office, 2026; European Commission, 2026; OpenAlex, 2026).

What happens

Identify the technology, related organisations and dated events, then connect research, intellectual property, funding, company, investment, collaboration, licensing and pilot records.

Output

A dated trajectory for each case showing the technology, actors, evidence sources and major transfer/evaluation milestones.

Sampling plan: develop a candidate pool across AI/software and intelligent systems; biotechnology, health and biomedical technologies; photonics and advanced materials; and energy and resource-efficient technologies. The final core sample will prioritise two or three domains based on traceable evaluation records, partner relevance and feasibility, while retaining the broader pool for comparison where data permit. This avoids promising equal coverage across domains before access is confirmed (Haessler et al., 2023; OECD, 2025). The proposal defines one technology opportunity followed from research origin through subsequent evaluation, transfer and commercialisation events as the unit of analysis.

Case identification and selection: The initial opportunity universe will be constructed using explicit inclusion criteria and a documented entity-resolution protocol linking publications, patent families, projects, organisations and commercialisation events. Ambiguous matches will be flagged instead of being automatically merged, with matching decisions, confidence levels and source provenance retained for audit. From this universe, trajectories will be selected to support comparison across technology families, application contexts and transfer stages, including contrasting cases in which opportunities progress, stall, are redirected or are abandoned. This combines systematic case construction with the contextual depth needed to explain heterogeneous technology-transfer processes (Cunningham et al., 2017; Beach, 2020).

What is collected

Time-stamped text from patents, publications, grants, TTO materials, venture/company pages, investment announcements and other accessible communications.

Evaluation events

TTO assessments, grant decisions, investor pitches, industry negotiations, pilots, licensing decisions and other moments where the opportunity is evaluated. TTOs can act as brokers across academic and industrial boundaries while also managing institutional and ownership constraints (Sapir, 2021; Chen et al., 2025).

What is coded

For each event: problem, value, evidence, uncertainty and the requested response. This keeps evaluation tied to observable interaction, not as a purely rhetorical property of framing (van Werven et al., 2019; Clarke & Llewellyn, 2026).

Key principle: the project uses contemporaneous, time-stamped material wherever available so that changes in an opportunity account can be observed, not reconstructed only from hindsight.

Three dimensions

Meaning: what the technology is understood to be. Value: why it is worth supporting or adopting. Uncertainty: what remains unknown or unvalidated.

Computational analysis

Transformer-based text representations and comparison of language patterns, complemented by transparent dictionary-based measures. The design is informed by research demonstrating how linguistic and narrative features can be analysed systematically in entrepreneurial settings (Pan et al., 2020; Oo et al., 2023).

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative coding and process tracing are used to interpret why language changes and to examine the context behind computational patterns.

Methodological logic: computational analysis identifies recurring or changing patterns; transparent coding and qualitative process tracing make those patterns interpretable and tied to specific evaluation episodes.

Interview sample

Approximately 35–50 interviews with researchers, entrepreneurs, TTO professionals, investors and industry partners. This multi-actor design reflects evidence that technology-transfer work involves brokerage, legitimacy management and competing organisational expectations across boundaries (O’Kane et al., 2015; Sapir, 2021).

Episode reconstruction

Link interview accounts and accessible interaction records to specific evaluation events identified in the trajectory.

Analytical question

Who challenged, accepted, questioned or revised an opportunity account — and what evidence or response followed? Recent research on investment pitches similarly treats evaluation as an interaction in which investors challenge and redirect entrepreneurial narratives, instead of simply receiving a fixed story (Clarke & Llewellyn, 2026).

Contribution: this step moves beyond observing that language changed. It reconstructs the interactional process through which an opportunity was interpreted and potentially reconfigured.

Design

Cross-case comparison examines how different representations of an opportunity appear in real evaluation encounters and how they relate to evidence requests, stakeholder interpretations and subsequent decisions (Pan et al., 2020; Clarke & Llewellyn, 2026).

Stakeholder groups

35–50 interviews with researchers, TTO professionals, entrepreneurs, investors and industry partners, subject to recruitment feasibility.

Measures

Cross-case patterns in meaning, perceived value, evidence sufficiency, uncertainty and transfer outcomes.

Purpose: cross-case comparison complements the longitudinal cases by identifying recurring and contrasting patterns while preserving the context of each evaluation encounter.

Observable outcomes

Funding, collaboration, licensing, pilots, spin-off development and abandonment, alongside patents, company and investment events where relevant.

Temporal linkage

Align changes in opportunity accounts and evaluation episodes with dated subsequent resource, relationship and transfer events.

Interpretation

Assess how patterns of reconfiguration are associated with subsequent trajectories without assuming that framing alone causes those outcomes.

Core question: under what conditions does adaptation improve audience relevance while preserving decision-relevant technological uncertainty?

04 · INTEGRATED RESEARCH WORKFLOW

One opportunity trajectory, three connected layers.

The work packages describe what the project does; the data spine defines how evidence stays connected; and the analysis plan specifies how each research question is answered. These are not three separate methods. They are one workflow: build traceable cases, reconstruct evaluation events, analyse how meaning/value/uncertainty change, and relate those changes to subsequent transfer trajectories without assuming causality.

WP1 · DATA FOUNDATION

Build the opportunity universe and data spine

Core question: What constitutes a traceable high-tech opportunity, and which observable records allow it to be followed across institutional boundaries?

Create the reproducible population from which trajectories are sampled and establish a common identifier system linking research, intellectual property, organisations, funding, firms and investment events.

Data integration and quality assurance. Record linkage will combine stable identifiers where available with documented matching rules for organisation names, inventor and author affiliations, patent families, project identifiers and company records. Each link will retain its source, matching method, confidence level and any manual review decision. The project will distinguish missing information from confirmed absence of an event and will maintain versioned records of extraction, cleaning, classification and coding. These procedures will make the opportunity register reproducible and allow uncertain links to be revisited as additional evidence becomes available.

  • Use EPO PATSTAT for patent families, applicants, inventors, classifications and legal/procedural events.
  • Use the EPO Deep Tech Finder as a discovery and cross-check layer, while retaining project-level entity resolution and provenance.
  • Use CORDIS for EU-funded projects, participating organisations, collaborations and project results.
  • Use OpenAlex and institutional repositories for publication metadata, authorship, affiliations, citations and topic representations.
  • Classify each candidate by a primary technology family and retain secondary technology tags to capture convergence.
  • Separately classify application/industry context so that technology and market setting are not conflated.

WP2 · LONGITUDINAL RECONSTRUCTION

Reconstruct technology-opportunity trajectories

Core question: How does the same opportunity acquire different meanings, value claims and uncertainty profiles as it travels through research, TTO, investor and industry settings, and does this reconfiguration vary by technology family or application context?

Turn heterogeneous records into event-level histories showing when an opportunity moved between communities of evaluation, what each community asked for, and what changed afterwards.

  • Collect publications, patents, grant applications/results, TTO materials, company descriptions, accessible pitch/investor materials, licensing or partnership announcements and pilot descriptions.
  • Record claim, audience, evidence, uncertainty, requested action and subsequent response for each item, with separate fields for technology family and application/industry context.
  • Keep publication date and event date separate to avoid retrospective accounts being treated as contemporaneous evidence.
  • Assign source reliability and access status.
OutputA justified core set of validated trajectories and an event-level corpus; the final sample size will be set after feasibility checks.
FeedsWP3 mechanism analysis and WP4 corpus-wide measurement.
WP3 · MECHANISMS

Explain meaning-making at evaluation boundaries

Core question: Who changes an opportunity account, why does the change occur, and which questions or evidence requirements trigger the revision?

Use interviews and close qualitative reconstruction to explain the interactional mechanisms behind observed changes. The analysis will also examine the documents, prototypes, evidence summaries and other boundary objects through which actors make an opportunity sufficiently understandable to evaluate, while recognising that shared artefacts do not automatically create shared interpretations (Caccamo et al., 2023).

Mechanism-focused analysis. Each selected episode will be reconstructed by comparing contemporaneous documents, interview accounts and observable subsequent responses. The analysis will trace the sequence from an initial opportunity claim, through a stakeholder question or objection, to any evidence request, revision of the opportunity account and subsequent evaluation response. Rival explanations—including changes in technical maturity, available resources, organisational priorities or external market conditions—will be considered alongside framing-related explanations. This process-tracing approach will help distinguish plausible mechanisms from simple temporal association (Beach, 2020).

  • Target approximately 35–50 interviews across researchers, entrepreneurs, TTO professionals, investors and industry partners, adjusting the final number to trajectory coverage and information power.
  • Use event-specific prompts anchored to identifiable evaluation episodes.
  • Connect participant-provided documents to trajectory records through study-specific event IDs.
  • Compare successful, delayed, redirected and abandoned trajectories across technology families and the priority industry/application contexts.
OutputMechanism-focused case reconstructions.
Analytical dimensionsMeaning · value · uncertainty · actor · audience · evidence request · response · outcome.
WP4 · COMPUTATIONAL COMPARISON

Measure patterns of framing, value and uncertainty at scale

Core question: Which linguistic and evidential patterns distinguish representations of the same opportunity across evaluation communities, technology families and application/industry contexts?

Move from close case reconstruction to systematic comparison across the corpus, testing whether opportunity accounts change predictably across audiences and stages of transfer.

Candidate measures. The possible features the study plans to measure include: (1) problem-framing indicators, such as the stated user need and application specificity; (2) value-claim categories, including technical, commercial, societal and environmental benefits; (3) evidence specificity, such as quantified results, benchmark comparisons, prototype tests and validation status; (4) uncertainty markers, including explicit limitations, hedging, unresolved questions and stated validation needs; (5) novelty and familiarity cues; and (6) requested next actions, such as further testing, funding, partnership, licensing or piloting. Changes between successive documents can be measured through coded category shifts and semantic-distance scores, with examples checked in context. Where multiple researchers code documents, agreement will be assessed on an overlapping subset and disagreements used to refine the codebook. Automated measures will be evaluated against human-coded examples and close readings, with sensitivity checks across alternative measures and document subsets. Text analysis will support systematic comparison across the corpus while retaining contextual interpretation (Grimmer & Stewart, 2013; O’Connor & Joffe, 2020). Structural topic modelling may be used exploratorily to examine how topic prevalence varies with document-level metadata (Roberts et al., 2019).

  • Combine transformer-based text embeddings with transparent dictionary and supervised coding measures.
  • Compare documents within trajectories over time and across the three priority industry/application contexts, with comparisons also made within trajectories over time.
  • Link language shifts to boundary crossings, evidence requests, investment events, partnerships, pilots and licensing.
  • Run sensitivity checks across document type, technology field, trajectory length, missingness and alternative definitions of frame adaptation.
OutputTransparent computational measurement framework with reproducible preprocessing and robustness checks.
GuardrailComputational similarity identifies patterns; it does not by itself establish mechanism or causality.
WP5 · CROSS-CASE COMPARISON

Compare how value and uncertainty are reconfigured

Core question: Across which evaluation settings and opportunity trajectories do changes in framing coincide with changes in perceived value, evidence requirements and subsequent decisions?

Compare cases across two or three feasible technology domains, paying attention to changes in problem definition, novelty, commercial potential, demonstrated performance, societal relevance and unresolved uncertainty. The analysis will examine contrasting trajectories, including opportunities that progress, stall, are redirected or are abandoned, without assuming that framing alone causes those outcomes (Dosi, 1982; Kaplan & Tripsas, 2008; Spieth et al., 2021).

Validation. Use negative cases, source triangulation, transparent coding rules and discussion of interim interpretations with the Belgian non-academic partner. Stakeholder feedback will help assess practical relevance but will remain analytically distinct from empirical findings.

OutputCross-case account of conditions under which opportunity adaptation supports evaluation or obscures unresolved uncertainty.
GuardrailPatterns and associations will not be presented as causal effects without appropriate evidence.
WP6 · INTEGRATION & DIAGNOSTIC

Link reconfiguration to transfer trajectories and build the diagnostic

Core question: Under what conditions is adaptation associated with subsequent transfer outcomes—funding, collaboration, licensing, piloting or spin-off development—without erasing decision-relevant uncertainty?

Integrate trajectory events, qualitative mechanisms, computational measures and exploratory quantitative estimates, where feasible, into a common evidence matrix. Interpretation will distinguish multiple routes to knowledge transfer and commercialisation, without assuming that patenting, licensing or spin-off creation alone captures the full value of university–industry engagement (Grimaldi et al., 2011; Howells, 2006).

Temporal and outcome interpretation. The analysis will distinguish the timing of framing changes from the timing of subsequent funding, collaboration, licensing, piloting and other transfer events. Where the number and quality of observations permit, trajectories will be compared by outcome type and stage, while accounting for differences in technology maturity, application context and evidence availability. Because successful transfer can take multiple forms and outcomes may be incompletely observed, the diagnostic will record both realised outcomes and unresolved or censored trajectories. Associations between framing adaptation and subsequent transfer will be interpreted in light of alternative explanations, not as evidence of a standalone causal effect.

  • Align frame-change dates with subsequent funding, investment, collaboration, licensing, pilots, spin-off development and abandonment where observable, treating these as longitudinal transfer outcomes, not direct experimental dependent variables.
  • Use temporal ordering to distinguish prior representations from later outcomes.
  • Compare trajectories with similar technologies or stages where feasible, including cases where translation occurred without a corresponding transfer outcome.
  • Produce a diagnostic recording audience, opportunity claim, evidence supplied, unresolved uncertainty, frame change, evaluation response and subsequent outcome.
OutputTechnology Opportunity Translation Diagnostic, papers, anonymised coding framework, stakeholder/policy brief and reusable dataset structure.
Final yearReplication checks, stakeholder validation, dissemination and data/code documentation.
Work-package logic

WP1 establishes the identifiers, technology-family classification and three-industry/application sampling frame. WP2 turns those identifiers into longitudinal event histories. WP3 explains how evaluation interactions produce revisions. WP4 tests whether framing, value and uncertainty patterns vary systematically within trajectories, across technology families and across application contexts. WP5 compares trajectories and tests the robustness of interpretations across cases; it does not assume a causal effect of opportunity framing. WP6 reconnects those experimental effects to longitudinal transfer outcomes without claiming that framing alone causes commercialisation (Haessler et al., 2023; Reynolds et al., 2025; Bunduchi & Candi, 2026).

ANALYSIS PLAN · THREE RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Use computational measures to guide, not replace, interpretation.

The computational workflow is designed to support the substantive research questions, not to substitute for interpretation. Automated outputs will be checked against human-coded examples and read in the context of each opportunity’s trajectory.

RQ1 · Evaluation

Which frames are used by each community?

Unit coded: claim- or sentence-level passage within a dated artefact. A document can contain more than one frame.

  • Multi-label categories: technological, commercial, societal and safety.
  • Annotate an initial target of approximately 500 artefacts, refine the codebook and assess agreement on an overlapping subset.
  • Train and compare supervised text classifiers; report per-class precision, recall and F1, plus calibration where appropriate.
  • Compare frame probabilities across actor roles, artefact types, domains and trajectory stages.

Output: a Frame Atlas showing how frame patterns differ across communities and over time.

RQ2 · Interaction

How do accounts change between encounters?

Unit compared: chronologically ordered artefacts and, where possible, claims about the same opportunity.

  • Represent artefacts or matched passages with sentence embeddings; compare adjacent events and cross-community pairs.
  • Use cosine distance as a semantic-change indicator, not as proof of adaptation by itself.
  • Separately code epistemic status: demonstrated, projected, hypothetical or uncertain.
  • Use interviews and process tracing to examine who revised an account, in response to which question or evidence request, and what happened next.

Output: event-level records of frame shifts, semantic changes, evidence requests and subsequent responses.

RQ3 · Consequences

When is adaptation associated with outcomes?

Unit of analysis: the technology opportunity, with time-ordered measures aggregated from its artefact trajectory.

  • Outcomes tracked separately: funding/investment, collaboration, licensing, pilots, venture formation and abandonment.
  • Begin with trajectory comparisons and process tracing; use parsimonious models only if the number and distribution of outcomes support them.
  • Potential covariates include technology domain, maturity/stage, prior funding and observation time, where reliable.
  • Do not treat hundreds of artefacts as hundreds of independent opportunities or interpret associations as causal effects.

Output: evidence on conditions associated with progress, redirection, delay or abandonment.

RESEARCH MODEL · IV / DV / CONTEXT

Framing is the intervention; stakeholder evaluation is the immediate outcome; transfer is the longitudinal outcome.

The project distinguishes the representation of an opportunity from what stakeholders subsequently evaluate and from what happens to the opportunity over time. This prevents the empirical design from treating “commercialisation” as a single short-term dependent variable. The experimental component follows best-practice guidance for vignette studies (Aguinis & Bradley, 2014) and draws on evidence that technology maturity, market-segment clarity and regulatory hurdles influence entrepreneurs’ technology-licensing evaluations (Brown et al., 2022).

Longitudinal DVTransfer trajectory
Funding, investment, collaboration, licensing, pilots, spin-off development, redirection or abandonment.
Industry/application context as moderator: the study focuses on three priority contexts—healthcare & medtech, digital/enterprise technologies, and advanced industrial/manufacturing systems. Technology family and technology maturity are additional contextual dimensions. The expectation is not that framing has one universal effect, but that its consequences depend on the evidentiary, institutional and market conditions in which an opportunity is evaluated (Pan et al., 2020; Bojovic, 2022; Raffaelli et al., 2019; Taeuscher & Rothe, 2024).
CORE TESTABLE RELATION

Holding technical content, maturity and evidence constant, different opportunity frames should change stakeholder evaluation. The magnitude and direction of this effect may vary by application context and technology characteristics; longitudinally, frame adaptation is expected to be associated with subsequent transfer trajectories when it responds to legitimate evidence and stakeholder requirements without merely suppressing uncertainty.

07 · IMPLEMENTATION

A feasible 36-month sequence.

The order front-loads access, definitions and annotation so that model choice and outcome analysis follow demonstrated data feasibility.

MONTHS 1–6

Scope and build the corpus

  • Confirm access, ethics, consent and data-management arrangements.
  • Build a candidate universe from OpenAlex, CORDIS, PATSTAT, EPO Deep Tech Finder and accessible VUB/TTO or company records.
  • Define inclusion criteria and entity-linking rules; select a feasible set of 30–40 opportunity trajectories across two or three priority domains.
Milestone: feasibility-reviewed opportunity register and initial data dictionary.
MONTHS 7–12

Define and validate measures

  • Develop the codebook for four frames, epistemic status and evidence support.
  • Manually annotate an initial target of around 500 artefacts, subject to corpus availability.
  • Assess inter-coder agreement; benchmark simple and transformer-based classifiers.
Milestone: documented annotation protocol and baseline model evaluation.
MONTHS 13–18

Reconstruct evaluation episodes

  • Start interviews with researchers, TTO professionals, entrepreneurs, investors and industry partners.
  • Link interview accounts to dated artefacts and evaluation events.
  • Trace objections, evidence requests and revisions in selected cases.
Milestone: first episode-level case reconstructions.
MONTHS 19–24

Complete fieldwork and comparisons

  • Work towards 35–50 interviews, adjusted to recruitment feasibility and case coverage.
  • Complete longitudinal comparisons across actors and opportunity stages.
  • Check negative cases and alternative explanations for observed changes.
Milestone: cross-case evidence matrix and validated qualitative interpretations.
MONTHS 25–30

Integrate measures and outcomes

  • Calculate frame and semantic-change measures on the validated corpus.
  • Link time-ordered measures to observable funding, collaboration, licensing, pilot and abandonment events.
  • Use exploratory statistical models only where outcome counts and data quality support them.
Milestone: integrated trajectory analysis and diagnostic prototype.
MONTHS 31–36

Validate, write and disseminate

  • Test the diagnostic with relevant stakeholders and record usability feedback.
  • Run robustness and replication checks; document codebook, limitations and data provenance.
  • Prepare research papers, a policy brief and reusable data structure where permissions allow.
Milestone: stakeholder-reviewed Technology Opportunity Translation Diagnostic.

Frame adaptation should matter most at boundary-crossing moments. The key empirical question is whether adaptation improves access to resources without reducing the visibility of technical uncertainty.

09 · IMPACT

From longitudinal evidence to a practical translation diagnostic.

Technology Opportunity Translation Diagnostic

Identify how an opportunity is understood by different stakeholders, which evidence supports its value claims, which uncertainties remain unresolved and where its representation changes.

For VUB TechTransfer

A longitudinal evidence base for understanding how opportunities are assessed, promoted, challenged and de-risked across transfer processes.

For researchers & entrepreneurs

A clearer account of how opportunity descriptions change as they move between communities of evaluation, without treating adaptation as equivalent to evidence.

For industry, investors & policy

Evidence on how evaluation contexts, relationships and uncertainty shape technology-transfer trajectories, with practical prompts for transparent assessment when evidence is incomplete.

Open-access papersStakeholder-tested diagnosticPolicy briefAnonymised coding framework where permittedDocumented data architecture
Policy focus: The policy brief will address how universities, public funders and innovation-support organisations can assess emerging technologies when evidence is incomplete, distinguish demonstrated capability from plausible future value, and define proportionate next-step evidence requirements. Recommendations will be grounded in the findings and co-developed with relevant stakeholders, not assumed in advance.

10 · WHY INTERACT / WHY VUB

The project is designed around InterACT’s interdisciplinary and intersectoral architecture.

Excellence

Connects entrepreneurship and technology-transfer research with organisational sensemaking, computational text analysis, qualitative process analysis and longitudinal case comparison.

Impact

Links opportunity evaluation to responsible knowledge valorisation and a policy brief for institutions that assess emerging technologies under uncertainty. The diagnostic recognises multiple pathways to knowledge transfer and does not treat patenting, licensing or spin-off creation as exhaustive indicators of success (Grimaldi et al., 2011; Howells, 2006).

Implementation

Build a traceable case base, explain evaluation encounters, compare trajectories and integrate findings. The final scope will be matched to verified data access and recruitment feasibility.

Co-creation

Co-creation has three stages: the Belgian non-academic partner helps define a practical evaluation challenge; stakeholders review emerging interpretations during the study; and partner representatives test the diagnostic against practical cases. The partner’s role is advisory and collaborative, while research independence and confidentiality are maintained.

INTERSECTORAL AND INTERNATIONAL COLLABORATION

Co-create the research, not only the final tool

Belgian non-academic secondment, Year 1: a 3–5 month placement with a Belgian non-academic organisation working in technology development, innovation support, industry collaboration or evidence-informed policy. A designated mentor would help define a practical evaluation challenge, provide tasks relevant to the host organisation and discuss emerging findings. The final host will be selected based on fit and a formal agreement; access to confidential records is not assumed.

International academic secondment, Year 2: a 3–5 month placement with an international group whose expertise complements the project in entrepreneurship, innovation evaluation, organisational sensemaking or technology transfer, preferably through EUTOPIA. This placement will strengthen the theoretical framework and support comparative interpretation.

Co-creation cycle: identify the practical challenge with stakeholders; discuss interim patterns and test interpretations; then refine the diagnostic and policy brief with feedback from relevant users. The project will distinguish stakeholder feedback from empirical findings and report disagreement transparently.

RESEARCH ENVIRONMENT

VUB Business Technology & Operations

The project sits closely with research on technology transfer, academic entrepreneurship, collaborative R&D and innovation ecosystems. The wider literature includes work on technology-transfer professionals as boundary brokers, TTO legitimacy, academic-entrepreneurship ecosystems, university startups and the changing institutional role of technology-transfer offices (O’Kane et al., 2015; Hayter et al., 2018; Sapir, 2021; Chen et al., 2025; Giunti & Duberley, 2026).

Crispeels et al. (2026), a recent contribution from VUB’s Business Technology & Operations research environment, is especially relevant because it examines university technology-transfer ecosystems through their micro-foundations: who participates, how actors navigate processes, and where these interactions take place. Its recommendation to investigate ecosystem navigation at a more granular level supports this project’s focus on how different communities evaluate and reconfigure the value of technology opportunities over time. This project complements that perspective by tracing how evaluations and value claims vary across actors, settings and stages of a technology-transfer trajectory.
